# pH in Hydroponics

## Why pH matters
pH controls how well plant roots can absorb nutrients from the solution. Most
hydroponic crops thrive between pH 5.5 and 6.5. Outside this range, key
nutrients like iron, phosphorus, and calcium become chemically "locked out"
and unavailable to the plant even if they are present in the reservoir —
causing deficiency symptoms despite correct nutrient dosing. Stable pH is one
of the single most important factors in hydroponic success, more important
day-to-day than exact nutrient concentration.

## How pH drifts over time
In a recirculating hydroponic system, pH naturally drifts as plants absorb
nutrients unevenly — uptake of ammonium nitrogen tends to push pH down, while
uptake of nitrate nitrogen tends to push it up. Evaporation concentrates the
solution and can also shift pH. This is why pH should be checked at least
daily, and ideally monitored continuously with a calibrated probe.

## Correcting high pH
If pH rises above the crop's ideal range, use a pH-down solution (typically
phosphoric or citric acid based) added in small increments, waiting at least
10-15 minutes between doses to let the solution fully mix before re-checking.
Large single doses risk overshooting into an equally harmful low-pH state.

## Correcting low pH
If pH falls below the ideal range, use a pH-up solution (typically potassium
hydroxide based) in the same small, incremental way. Always dose gradually —
target the midpoint of the crop's ideal range rather than swinging to the
opposite extreme.

## Calibrating your pH probe
pH probes drift over time and should be calibrated against reference buffer
solutions (commonly pH 4.0, 7.0, and 10.0) every one to two weeks. An
uncalibrated probe is one of the most common causes of "mystery" plant stress
in otherwise well-managed systems.
